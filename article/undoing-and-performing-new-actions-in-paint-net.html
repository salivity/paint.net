Undoing and Performing New Actions in Paint.NET

When working in Paint.NET, performing an undo operation steps backward through your project's edit history, allowing you to review or revert recent changes. However, if you perform a new action immediately after undoing, all subsequent "future" history steps that were available to redo are permanently deleted. This article explains how Paint.NET processes this action, why those future states are removed, and how to avoid losing unwanted work.

The Linear History Model

Paint.NET relies on a strictly linear history stack, which is visible in the History window (accessible via F3 or the History icon in the top-right corner). Every stroke, adjustment, layer creation, and selection is logged sequentially from top to bottom.

When you click Undo (or press Ctrl + Z), the active state moves up the list. The steps you reversed remain visible in the History window as grayed-out items. At this stage, you can still step forward using Redo (or Ctrl + Y) to restore those actions.

What Happens When You Perform a New Action

The moment you make a new edit on the canvas—such as drawing a line, applying a filter, adding a new layer, or pasting content—while positioned at an earlier point in the history:

  1. Immediate Truncation: Paint.NET considers the new edit as the start of an entirely new path. Everything located below the current step in the History window is instantly wiped out.
  2. Permanent Loss of Redo States: The previously grayed-out "future" steps cease to exist. They are purged from application memory and cannot be recovered via the Redo command.
  3. Continuation of the New Timeline: The new edit becomes the latest entry immediately following the step you reverted to, establishing a new linear timeline.

Why Paint.NET Handles History This Way

Paint.NET does not utilize non-linear history branching or snapshot trees. Maintaining alternative branches of image edits requires significant memory overhead, especially when working with high-resolution, multi-layered canvases. By truncating future steps, the application frees up RAM and keeps history tracking simple and predictable.

How to Preserve Work Before Testing Changes

If you want to step backward to try a different editing approach without losing your forward progress:

  • Save Multiple Iterations: Before undoing, save your work under a new filename using File > Save As (Ctrl + Shift + S) in the native .pdn format. This preserves the current visual state in a separate file.
  • Duplicate Layers: Instead of undoing operations on a specific layer, duplicate the layer beforehand. You can hide the original layer to preserve your progress while experimenting on the duplicate.
